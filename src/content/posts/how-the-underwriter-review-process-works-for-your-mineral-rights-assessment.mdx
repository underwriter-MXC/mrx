## Answer first

An underwriter review can be organized around the records available, public production and well-information context, stated assumptions, and written questions. It should also make clear what it does not determine: ownership, title, legal consequences, tax consequences, or a certified appraisal result.

![MRX document review workspace labeled “free mineral rights review”.](/assets/articles/inline/how-the-underwriter-review-process-works-for-your-mineral-rights-assessment/free-mineral-rights-review.webp)

## Step 1: define the interest and the question

Start by identifying the property and the mineral interest the discussion concerns. Ask what question the review is intended to help you understand and what documentation would be useful to that conversation. Do not assume that a review resolves title or confirms ownership.

The [Texas Comptroller's Property Classification Guide](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/property-tax/docs/96-313.pdf) distinguishes surface and oil-and-gas/mineral interests for classification purposes. That is helpful general context, not a determination about a particular owner's records.

## Step 2: identify public operational context

The Texas RRC offers [Oil and Gas Production Data](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/oil-and-gas/research-and-statistics/production-data/) and [Well Information](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/oil-and-gas/research-and-statistics/well-information/) resources. An owner can use those records to ask which reported production or well-status information is relevant to the discussion.

Public RRC records are not title records. They provide reported production and well-information context only, so they should not be used to make ownership or value conclusions by themselves.

## Step 3: separate facts, assumptions, and boundaries

Ask the reviewer to distinguish reported information from assumptions and to explain any dates, gaps, or unresolved matters. A clear boundary statement can help an owner decide whether to obtain legal, tax, title, or certified-appraisal advice before acting.

## Step 4: read proposed terms separately

If a transaction is being discussed, request written terms and identify contingencies, adjustments, and questions that could affect your decision. A review process is more understandable when its informational discussion is not confused with the terms of a potential transaction.

## Questions to bring to a review

- What property and interest are being discussed?
- Which records and reporting periods were reviewed?
- What information is assumed or unconfirmed?
- Which issues are outside the review's scope?
- What should I take to an appropriate outside professional?

## Frequently asked questions

### What can RRC production data tell me?

It can provide reported production context that may help you ask questions about inputs used in a review. It does not establish title, ownership, or a dollar value.

### Is a review a title opinion?

No. Questions about title or ownership require the relevant records and, where appropriate, qualified professional advice.

### Does a review guarantee a transaction result?

No. A review may help explain information and assumptions, but it does not guarantee a result or establish a required price.

### What should I do with an offer?

Read the written terms, ask what facts and assumptions underlie the discussion, and obtain professional advice when your circumstances require it.
