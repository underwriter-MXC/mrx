> **Comparison-only boundary.** This article explains how to compare the labels and disclosed assumptions in dated public oil and gas price-deck publications. It does not create, extend, convert, normalize, select, validate, or recommend a price deck; forecast commodity prices; calculate present value or mineral-rights value; interpret a reserve report or private instrument; or provide owner-specific engineering, appraisal, investment, legal, or tax guidance. MRX may have an economic interest in a later transaction; when that applies, the buyer relationship is disclosed in writing before an agreement is signed.

To **compare public oil and gas price decks**, place each publication in its own source column. Before looking at displayed prices, record the publisher, publication date, forecast-completion or effective period, version, commodity, benchmark, geographic basis, unit, nominal-or-real basis, time interval, horizon, stated use case, source URL, and retrieval date.

Compare only fields that have the same disclosed frame. If a field is absent or unclear, write `not stated` or `unresolved`. Do not fill the gap from another publication, convert a unit, extend a horizon, or silently substitute a newer version.

## What this comparison does and what it does not do

A public deck comparison is a source-control exercise. It answers a narrow question: are the displayed observations framed the same way?

That is different from the [one-variable sensitivity worksheet](/blog/mineral-valuation-sensitivity-analysis-one-variable-scenario-worksheet/), which records an output supplied by an existing model while one documented input changes. It is also different from a [discount-rate review](/blog/discount-rates-in-mineral-rights-dcf-a-plain-language-guide/), a property-level cash-flow forecast, an appraisal, or an offer analysis.

The comparison should not produce a blended price series. It should not decide that one publisher is more accurate, select a “conservative” case, or claim that a public outlook equals the price an owner will realize.

## Preserve the publication identity first

The [EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/outlook.php) is a useful example of why source identity matters. Its live page identifies a release date and forecast-completion date and provides access to current tables, data, and prior releases. A later release can change the displayed outlook while keeping a similar page title.

For each source, preserve:

- exact publication title and publisher;
- publication, release, or issue date;
- forecast-completion, data-through, effective, or reporting period when stated;
- edition, vintage, version, table, and page or section;
- stable URL or saved source file;
- access or retrieval date; and
- whether the source is a forecast, historical series, reserve-reporting method, scenario, survey, or another stated publication type.

Do not overwrite the publication date with the retrieval date. Do not cite only a landing page when the compared field came from a dated table or PDF; retain the table or file location too.

## Build a field-by-field matrix

Use one row per comparison field and one column per source. A practical matrix looks like this:

| Comparison field | Source A | Source B | Review note |
| ---------------- | -------- | -------- | ----------- |
| Publication identity | Exact title, publisher, version | Exact title, publisher, version | Same source type or different? |
| Dates | Release, completion, effective, and retrieval dates | Release, completion, effective, and retrieval dates | Do not collapse different dates |
| Commodity | Oil, natural gas, NGL, or another label exactly as stated | Exact stated commodity | Do not combine commodities |
| Benchmark or series | Exact named benchmark or source series | Exact named benchmark or source series | `not stated` if absent |
| Geographic basis | Country, region, hub, basin, delivery point, or other stated basis | Exact stated basis | Do not infer property location |
| Unit | Exact currency and physical unit | Exact currency and physical unit | Do not convert inside this matrix |
| Dollar basis | Nominal, real, constant-dollar base period, or `not stated` | Exact stated basis | Keep base periods visible |
| Time interval | Daily, monthly, quarterly, annual, average, or another label | Exact stated interval | Do not compare mismatched intervals as identical |
| Horizon | First and last displayed periods | First and last displayed periods | Do not extend missing periods |
| Stated use case | Publisher's stated purpose | Publisher's stated purpose | Keep forecast and disclosure rules separate |
| Assumptions and caveats | Exact section or concise sourced paraphrase | Exact section or concise sourced paraphrase | Preserve revision and uncertainty language |

The review-note column records only whether the frames match, conflict, or remain unresolved. It does not rate the sources or choose a winner.

## Keep commodity, benchmark, and geography separate

“Oil” and “natural gas” are not complete comparison labels. Record the commodity and the named benchmark or series separately. Then record the location or geographic basis stated by the publisher.

A public benchmark is not automatically a property's realized price. Location, quality, timing, contract terms, transportation, and other property-level facts may differ. The [basis-differentials guide](/blog/basis-differentials-and-mineral-rights-cash-flow/) owns that separate market-versus-property distinction; this article only preserves the source labels.

If Source A names a national or benchmark series and Source B names a regional hub, mark the geographic basis `different`. Do not bridge the difference with an assumed differential.

## Compare units before displayed values

EIA's [units and calculators overview](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/units-and-calculators/) explains that energy sources use different physical units and that conversion requires a defined method. Its [natural-gas units FAQ](https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=1417&t=7) distinguishes abbreviations such as cubic feet, Mcf, Btu, and MMBtu.

Copy the unit exactly as displayed, including the currency, denominator, and any scale prefix. For example, a per-barrel oil label and a per-MMBtu gas label belong in different commodity rows. A per-Mcf field and a per-MMBtu field should remain visibly different even when a separate analyst could convert them with additional assumptions.

Do not perform that conversion in the source matrix. A silent conversion can introduce a heat-content assumption, scale error, currency error, or base-period mismatch that the original publication never supplied.

## Record nominal or real dollar basis

EIA's [nominal and real prices FAQ](https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=13&t=2) says its published prices are generally nominal unless otherwise noted and explains that real prices are adjusted for inflation. That distinction belongs in its own row.

Record one of these labels only when the source supports it:

- `nominal`;
- `real`, with the stated base period;
- another explicit constant-dollar convention; or
- `not stated`.

Do not call a series real because it spans several years. Do not apply an inflation index, choose a base year, or restate one publication to match another inside this workflow. Those are new analytical choices and require separate documentation.

## Do not mix forecast decks with reserve-pricing rules

The SEC's [Modernization of Oil and Gas Reporting final rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2009-01-14/pdf/E9-409.pdf) describes a 12-month average pricing mechanism for specified oil-and-gas reserve disclosures. It also discusses disclosure context and pricing mechanisms. That prescribed reporting frame is not interchangeable with a publisher's future commodity outlook.

Label the source type before comparing it:

- public forecast or outlook;
- historical observation or spot-price series;
- scenario publication;
- reserve-disclosure pricing method;
- company or third-party planning deck; or
- `not stated`.

If two documents serve different stated purposes, the matrix should show `different use case`. Do not splice periods from one into the other or describe the result as a single public price deck.

## Use neutral status labels

Give each comparison row one status:

- `matched frame` when the disclosed commodity, benchmark, geography, unit, dollar basis, interval, horizon, and use case align;
- `different frame` when one or more disclosed fields differ;
- `missing` when a cited field or referenced document is unavailable; or
- `unresolved` when the available source does not support a reliable classification.

These labels describe the source frame only. `Matched frame` does not mean the displayed values are accurate, likely, appropriate for a property, or suitable for a decision. `Different frame` does not mean either source is wrong.

## Ask source questions instead of filling gaps

Convert every gap into a neutral question:

- “Which dated edition supplied this field?”
- “What forecast-completion or data-through date applies?”
- “Which commodity, benchmark, and geographic basis does this row use?”
- “Is the displayed unit per barrel, per Mcf, per MMBtu, or another stated unit?”
- “Are the dollar amounts nominal or real, and what base period applies?”
- “Is this publication a forecast, historical series, reserve-reporting method, or internal planning case?”
- “Which source states the horizon and update convention?”

Do not answer a missing field with a value from a different publisher or vintage. Preserve the gap so a reviewer can see exactly what remains unsupported.

## Stop before property-level analysis

The source matrix is complete when each publication has a stable identity and each comparison row has a copied field, source location, and status. Stop if the next step would require you to:

- create, extend, blend, interpolate, escalate, convert, or normalize a price series;
- select a base, upside, downside, conservative, or recommended deck;
- forecast oil, natural-gas, or NGL prices;
- apply a basis differential, quality adjustment, transportation assumption, or realized-price bridge;
- combine a deck with production, decline, development timing, costs, ownership, or discounting;
- calculate cash flow, present value, mineral-rights value, an offer, or a transaction result; or
- reach a conclusion about engineering, reserves, appraisal, investment, legal, or tax matters.

Those tasks require a separately controlled analysis and may call for a qualified petroleum engineer, reserves evaluator, appraiser, accountant, attorney, tax professional, financial professional, or other appropriate specialist. Give the reviewer the original publications and the completed comparison matrix, not only a blended summary.

## A compact quality check

Before sharing the comparison, confirm that:

- every source has an exact title, publisher, date, version, location, and retrieval date;
- each source remains in its own column;
- commodity, benchmark, geography, unit, dollar basis, interval, horizon, and use case are separate rows;
- absent fields say `not stated`, `missing`, or `unresolved`;
- no unit conversion, inflation adjustment, interpolation, extension, or normalization was added;
- forecast, historical, scenario, and reserve-reporting sources are not mislabeled as interchangeable; and
- the document contains no property-level price forecast, value, offer, or recommendation.

That discipline makes the comparison reproducible. It does not make a public deck property-specific, and it does not determine what a mineral interest is worth.
