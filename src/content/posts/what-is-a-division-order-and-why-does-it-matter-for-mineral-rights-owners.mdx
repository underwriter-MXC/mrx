## Answer first

A division order is a document that directs the payor how to distribute proceeds from identified oil or gas production among payees. It commonly lists the owner, property, decimal interest, effective date, payment address, tax information, and signature terms.

Texas Natural Resources Code section 91.401 defines division-order and payment terms, and section 91.402 addresses payment timing and permitted conditions. A form still deserves a line-by-line review. **Do not treat the decimal or property schedule as self-proving simply because it came from the operator or payor.**

## What a division order does

After a well or unit begins producing, the party responsible for distributing proceeds must identify payees and calculate their shares. The division order records the payor's current distribution instruction for a specific property or stream of production.

It can help establish an owner account and reduce uncertainty about where funds should be sent. It does not replace the recorded deeds, probate instruments, lease, amendments, pooled-unit records, or qualified title analysis underlying the payment setup.

## Verify six fields before signing

### 1. Payee identity

Confirm the legal owner or entity name. An inherited interest may require estate, trust, or transfer documents before the payor can change the account. A nickname, former name, or outdated entity can create tax-reporting or payment problems.

### 2. Property identity

Match the operator, lease, well, unit, county, and identifying numbers to the correct tract. Owners with several properties should not assume one division order covers all wells or products.

### 3. Decimal interest

Compare the stated decimal with the deed or probate interest, lease royalty, unit allocation, prior division orders, and recent statements. This is a reconciliation exercise, not a do-it-yourself title opinion.

### 4. Effective date

Identify when the instruction starts and whether it is prospective, retroactive, or tied to a transfer date. Ask how prior periods, suspense, adjustments, or duplicate payments will be handled.

### 5. Payment and tax information

Use the payor's verified delivery channel for addresses, banking instructions, and taxpayer information. Confirm separately before sending sensitive information in response to an unsolicited email or altered form.

### 6. Added language

Read certifications, indemnity, repayment, warranty, change-notice, governing-law, venue, electronic-payment, and amendment language. Texas statutory provisions do not make every added clause harmless.

## How to test the decimal without declaring title

Create a worksheet with separate inputs:

- tract or unit identification;
- stated ownership fraction;
- lease royalty fraction;
- unit or tract participation factor;
- product or well scope;
- effective date; and
- source document for each input.

The worksheet may reveal that two records use different tracts, leases, depths, or dates. It cannot determine which instrument legally controls. Use qualified help when a discrepancy is material.

The companion guide on [net mineral acres versus royalty acres](/blog/net-mineral-acres-vs-royalty-acres-what-texas-mineral-rights-owners-need-to-know/) explains why an acreage term cannot replace the decimal analysis.

## What to do when the form does not match

Do not silently change the form and assume the payor accepted the revision. Contact the verified owner-relations or division-order department and request the calculation support, title requirement, or correction process in writing.

Keep:

1. the original and signed forms;
2. delivery evidence;
3. deeds, leases, amendments, and unit records;
4. payor correspondence;
5. royalty statements before and after the change; and
6. any suspense or adjustment explanation.

The [Railroad Commission royalties FAQ](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/royalties-faq/) explains that the agency does not settle private royalty disputes. A disagreement may need to be addressed with the payor and qualified professional assistance.
