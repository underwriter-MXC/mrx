> **Educational valuation boundary.** This article explains a high-level directional model. It does not establish title, ownership, net mineral acres, royalty decimal, reserves, geology, a property-specific production forecast, realized prices, deductions, tax treatment, certified value, offer fairness, or whether to sign, sell, or hold. Use qualified title, land, legal, tax, accounting, appraisal, engineering, geology, and financial professionals for owner-specific conclusions. MRX may have an economic interest in a later transaction; when that applies, the buyer relationship is disclosed in writing before an agreement is signed.

Mineral rights are valued by translating an identified economic interest and a set of stated assumptions into conditional future royalty cash flows, discounting those cash flows to present value, and checking the result against relevant market evidence. The result is better understood as a range than as a single unexplained number because production, prices, timing, development, deductions, and even the reviewed interest may remain uncertain.

That sentence describes a model, not a property conclusion. A valuation can only be as specific as the evidence it receives. Missing or unresolved inputs should remain visible rather than being converted into implied facts.

## Start with the economic interest being modeled

Before projecting cash flow, define the interest the model has been asked to analyze. That description might include the tract or producing property, the stated ownership fraction, the stated royalty burden, the depths or formations identified in the supplied records, and whether the reviewed evidence concerns producing or undeveloped rights.

This is an input-definition step. It does not establish title, ownership, net mineral acres, or a legally correct royalty decimal. If those items are uncertain, the model should label them as owner-supplied, document-derived, assumed, or unresolved. A qualified title or land professional must resolve questions that require an ownership conclusion.

Keeping that boundary visible prevents a common analytical mistake: producing a precise-looking range from an interest that was never precisely identified.

## Organize production and royalty evidence

For a producing interest, the evidence set may include owner royalty statements, division-order materials, lease provisions available for review, and public production history. Each source answers a different question.

The Railroad Commission of Texas says its [production-data resources](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/oil-and-gas/research-and-statistics/production-data/) compile information reported by Texas operators. Its [Production Data Query FAQs](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/oil-gas-faq/production-data-query-system-faqs/) explain important limits: reporting can lag, later reports may revise earlier data, oil reporting is generally organized by lease, and gas leases may be organized differently. Public history is therefore useful evidence, but it is not automatically current, final, or matched to an individual owner's proceeds.

Royalty statements can help connect reported sales, realized prices, deductions, and the decimal applied to a payment period. They also require care. A statement displays what was reported or paid for that period; it does not by itself prove title, future terms, reserve quantities, or a legally correct decimal.

Normalize the evidence into consistent time periods and units. Preserve the source location and observation date for every important input. If records conflict, show the conflict as an unresolved item rather than silently choosing the value that produces the preferred result.

## Forecast production under stated assumptions

A producing-property model estimates how production may change over time. Decline-curve analysis uses observed production behavior to estimate parameters for a conditional forecast. The U.S. Energy Information Administration's [production decline-curve analysis](https://www.eia.gov/analysis/drilling/curve_analysis/2020/) describes how observed well-level data can be used to estimate decline parameters and project production.

That general method does not establish the future production of a specific property. Data quality, the period selected, downtime, maintenance, operating decisions, well work, commodity conditions, and model choice may affect the result. A transparent review states the history used, the forecast method, the effective date, and the assumptions that remain uncertain.

For undeveloped rights, future activity should not be treated as certain. A directional model may separate producing cash flow from an explicitly conditional undeveloped scenario supported by stated development evidence and timing assumptions. It should not label speculative inventory as proved reserves or present a geology or engineering conclusion without qualified work.

## Translate forecast production into royalty cash flow

At a high level, a royalty cash-flow model connects four categories:

1. forecast production for the modeled period;
2. a stated realized-price assumption;
3. the stated royalty or decimal interest applied to proceeds; and
4. deductions, taxes, or other adjustments supported by the reviewed terms and payment evidence.

The relationship can be described symbolically as forecast production multiplied by an assumed realized price, adjusted for the stated royalty economics and supported deductions. The exact implementation depends on the records and purpose of the review.

Every category should carry a source label. A benchmark price is not necessarily the price realized by the interest. A lease clause is not a conclusion about how a particular deduction must be treated. A decimal shown on a statement is not a title opinion. When the evidence does not establish an input, the model should present it as an assumption or an unresolved question.

## Discount conditional cash flows to present value

Cash expected farther in the future is not treated as equivalent to cash available now. A discounted-cash-flow model applies a stated discount rate to each modeled period and sums the resulting present values. The Texas Comptroller's [oil and gas mineral property appraisal material](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/property-tax/reappraisals/ector15-16.pdf) provides public-sector context for discounted cash flow and present-value analysis in mineral-property appraisal.

The discount rate does not erase uncertainty. It is an assumption that affects how the model translates timing and risk into present value. A useful review identifies the rate, effective date, forecast horizon, treatment of later cash flow, and any separate scenario assumptions. It does not hide those choices behind a single output.

This article does not audit which endpoint, rate, or sensitivity width is appropriate for a particular interest. The separate [range-width guide](/blog/why-is-my-mineral-rights-valuation-range-so-wide/) owns the job of checking why a range is broad and which assumptions move it.

## Use market evidence as a reasonableness check

Market evidence can test whether the income-model result appears directionally plausible. A useful comparison needs more than a price label. The compared interests should be evaluated for differences in production status, timing, royalty economics, deductions, acreage basis, location, development evidence, effective date, and transaction terms.

Because private transactions may disclose incomplete terms, market evidence should not automatically replace the cash-flow model. In this primer, it is a reasonableness check. A comparison that cannot be normalized should remain weak or excluded rather than being presented as a precise comparable.

An offer is also not neutral proof of value. It may reflect one buyer's assumptions, timing, diligence, contract terms, and objectives. Comparing an offer to a directional range can be informative, but the offer does not validate the model and the model does not establish that the offer is fair.

## Present a range with assumptions and limits

The output should connect each endpoint to a coherent set of assumptions. That might mean a narrower set of producing-cash-flow cases, separate conditional treatment for undeveloped potential, and a written list of unresolved inputs. The label attached to each case should explain what changed; it should not imply certainty.

A concise directional deliverable should identify:

- the economic interest described by the reviewed evidence;
- the production and royalty records used and their effective dates;
- the forecast method and period;
- stated price, deduction, timing, and discount assumptions;
- any conditional undeveloped scenario;
- the market evidence used only as a check;
- unresolved or owner-supplied inputs; and
- the professional and decision limits of the review.

## A practical review sequence

When reading a directional valuation, use this short sequence:

1. Confirm what economic interest the model says it analyzes.
2. Identify which production, royalty, lease, and development records were used.
3. Separate source-derived inputs from owner statements, model assumptions, and unresolved items.
4. Follow the conditional production forecast into royalty cash flow.
5. Confirm how timing and the stated discount rate translate cash flow to present value.
6. Check how undeveloped potential was separated and conditioned.
7. Treat market evidence as a reasonableness check, not automatic proof.
8. Read the range together with its effective date, assumptions, and limits.

If any link in that sequence is missing, the next step is clarification or qualified review, not false precision. A transparent range can support an informed discussion. It cannot replace the owner-specific title, engineering, appraisal, legal, tax, and transaction work that the situation may require.
