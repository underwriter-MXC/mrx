## Answer first

There is no standard number of days for selling mineral rights in Texas. A sale can move only as quickly as the parties can identify the property and interest, verify authority to convey, resolve the buyer's diligence questions, agree on complete terms, and coordinate signing, payment, deed delivery, and recording.

The most useful planning approach is therefore not to rely on a promised closing date. Map the required work, name who controls each step, and put the consequences of delay or a failed condition in writing.

## The six stages that control timing

### 1. Define the property and the interest

The seller and buyer need a common description of the county, tract, legal description, interest type, fraction, depth or formation limits, and whether the proposed sale is full or partial. A street address, operator name, or royalty-check decimal alone may not define the interest being conveyed.

### 2. Assemble ownership and payment records

Useful records can include prior deeds, probate or inheritance documents, leases, amendments, division orders, recent royalty statements, tax forms, and existing offers. Missing records do not automatically prevent a transaction, but they can create additional title or curative questions.

### 3. Conduct buyer diligence

A buyer may compare ownership documents with county records and review lease, production, royalty, well, and operator information. The [Railroad Commission of Texas](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/oil-and-gas/research-and-statistics/production-data/) describes its production information as data reported by operators. The Commission also cautions that production records are a snapshot and may change when revised, corrected, or late reports arrive.

Those public records are context. They do not establish the seller's title, net mineral acreage, royalty decimal, lease burden, or the value of a particular interest.

### 4. Resolve price and contract conditions

An offer may change if diligence confirms a different ownership fraction, net acreage, decimal, or property scope. Timing can also depend on exclusivity, extensions, termination rights, assignment, title objections, curative work, and the buyer's discretion to accept or reject an exception.

Before agreeing to a deadline, ask what remains conditional and which party can extend or end the process.

### 5. Execute and fund the closing

Texas Property Code section 5.021 states the general written, signed, and delivered requirements for a conveyance of an estate in real property. The actual instrument, acknowledgment, delivery mechanics, and payment instructions should be reviewed for the specific transaction.

A practical closing plan should state:

1. which agreement and conveyance will be signed;
2. who must sign and how signatures will be acknowledged;
3. when the deed is released or delivered;
4. when funds become available to the seller;
5. who may record the instrument; and
6. what happens if a closing condition is not satisfied.

### 6. Record and complete post-closing notices

Texas Property Code chapter 12 describes when an instrument concerning real property may be recorded. Recording is one part of the post-closing trail; depending on the transaction, the parties may also need to notify payors, retain the final settlement package, and route tax questions to a qualified adviser.

## Issues that commonly extend a sale

- An inherited interest lacks a complete probate or deed trail.
- The seller's name differs across deeds, division orders, or payment records.
- Multiple owners or fiduciaries must approve and sign.
- The legal description or depth language is unclear.
- The parties disagree about net mineral acres, royalty decimal, or the interest included.
- The buyer proposes a price adjustment that the seller has not accepted.
- Curative documents, releases, affidavits, or certified records are requested.
- The agreement gives one party broad extension or termination rights.
- Payment, delivery, and recording instructions do not align.

None of these issues proves that a transaction is defective. Each one is a reason to identify the responsible person, required evidence, and decision date.

## A seller-controlled readiness checklist

1. List every tract and interest you intend to include, along with anything you intend to retain.
2. Gather the documents you have without representing that they prove title.
3. Ask the buyer for a written diligence and closing checklist.
4. Require every price adjustment and deduction to be defined in writing.
5. Confirm who can extend, terminate, or assign the agreement.
6. Match deed delivery to verified payment instructions.
7. Keep the signed agreement, conveyance, settlement statement, payment evidence, and recording information together.
8. Use qualified legal, tax, title, or valuation help when the issue requires it.
