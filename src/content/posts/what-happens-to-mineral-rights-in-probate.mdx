> This article provides general Texas owner education. It is not a title opinion, probate strategy, fiduciary instruction, or determination of heirs or ownership shares. It does not provide owner-specific legal or tax guidance. A Texas probate attorney and qualified tax professional should apply current law to the documents.

## Answer first

**Texas mineral rights remain part of the decedent's property story after death, but the correct transfer and administration path must be documented.** The will, intestacy rules, trust or survivorship documents, court proceedings, county records, leases, and payor files may all affect what happens next.

The practical work is to prove authority, inventory every interest, preserve income, connect estate records to county title, and give operators or payors only the documents appropriate to the resolved legal path.

## Identify the governing estate path

Start with the decedent's residence, date of death, will, trusts, deeds, marital and family facts, and any open court proceeding. A mineral interest might pass under a probated will, intestacy, a trust, a survivorship arrangement, a prior deed, a small-estate procedure, or another instrument.

[Texas Estates Code Chapter 101](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/ES/pdf/ES.101.pdf) addresses estate assets and vesting at death subject to administration and applicable law. [Chapter 201](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/ES/pdf/ES.201.pdf) supplies intestate descent rules. Those statutes do not let an owner infer the correct heirs or shares without applying the actual family, property, debt, and instrument facts.

## Prove who may act

Determine whether a personal representative has been appointed and which powers apply. If a will is involved, [Texas Estates Code Chapter 256](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/ES/pdf/ES.256.pdf) governs probate of wills generally. Other paths may involve heirship, a small-estate affidavit under [Chapter 205](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/ES/pdf/ES.205.pdf), independent administration, or no court administration.

Do not assume that being a family member or receiving a royalty check creates authority to sign a lease, deed, division order, title affidavit, settlement, or sale contract. Ask counsel which person may act and which court, deed, or other evidence must support that action.

## Build a complete mineral inventory

Create one row per tract or interest with:

- county, survey, abstract, section, block, tract, and legal description;
- deed, reservation, assignment, will, probate, heirship, or trust source;
- claimed mineral, royalty, overriding royalty, leasehold, or other interest;
- gross acreage and claimed net ownership;
- leases, amendments, units, wells, depths, and formations;
- operators, payors, owner numbers, and suspense notices; and
- producing, nonproducing, leased, or unleased status.

Use royalty statements and tax forms as discovery evidence. Mark conflicts rather than resolving them by guess. A payment record can reveal a lease or well, but it may not describe every tract or the full ownership fraction.

## Preserve income while title is updated

Notify the appropriate advisers and determine how payors should be contacted. Keep copies of every request and document transmitted. Use contact information independently verified from the operator or payor, and redact unrelated sensitive data.

Track royalties paid before and after death, suspense amounts, tax withholding, deductions, adjustments, estate expenses, and distributions. Ask the tax professional which income belongs to the decedent's final return, the estate, a trust, or beneficiaries.

## Connect probate evidence to county title

Probate and county records serve different functions. Counsel may recommend recording a certified probate document, heirship evidence, deed, distribution instrument, or another document in each county where the mineral interest is located. Multi-county ownership can require repeated recording and payor packets.

Do not record an informal family spreadsheet or a document copied from another estate without review. Names, legal descriptions, capacities, acknowledgment, and property language need to match the actual title and estate path.

## Treat mineral decisions as fiduciary decisions

Estate property may require leasing, pooling, division-order review, title cure, operation, sale, or distribution. [Texas Estates Code Chapter 358](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/ES/pdf/ES.358.pdf) addresses particular matters relating to mineral properties in estate administration. It does not authorize every person or every transaction automatically.

Before signing, document who has authority, which interest is affected, why the action is needed, which valuation or market evidence was considered, which conflicts exist, and how proceeds will be held or distributed.

## Preserve basis and tax administration records

[IRS Publication 559](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p559) provides federal guidance for survivors, executors, and administrators. Preserve date-of-death valuation support, prior acquisition and basis records, depletion history, statements, tax forms, expenses, estate income, distributions, and later transaction records.

A probate inventory value, property-tax value, offer, royalty multiple, and credentialed valuation can serve different purposes. Ask the tax professional which valuation date, standard, and documentation apply.

## Close with a successor operating file

When the legal path is resolved, update the tract schedule, county recording references, payor contacts, owner numbers, beneficiary records, tax file, and pending action list. Give each successor enough information to continue administration without exposing passwords, full account numbers, or unrelated personal data.
