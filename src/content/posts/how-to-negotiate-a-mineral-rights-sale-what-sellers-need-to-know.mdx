## Answer first

To negotiate a mineral-rights sale, negotiate the complete exchange: the exact interest being conveyed, the calculation of expected net proceeds, the buyer's adjustment rights, diligence and exclusivity, closing and payment mechanics, assignment, and every representation or obligation that survives closing.

A counteroffer is strongest when it points to a specific clause or business term and proposes a clear alternative. “Increase the price” is less complete than “use this defined interest, this adjustment method, this diligence period, and these closing instructions.”

MRX provides directional underwriter reviews and may become a buyer in some transactions. If MRX may be the buyer, that relationship is disclosed before an agreement is signed. Apply the same negotiation framework to an MRX proposal or any other buyer's proposal.

## Start by defining the asset

Before negotiating price, confirm that both parties are discussing the same property and rights. Identify the county, legal description, interest type, ownership fraction, net mineral acres if applicable, depth or formation limits, effective date, and whether the sale is full or partial.

Pay attention to broad phrases such as “all interests owned,” after-acquired interests, executive rights, royalties, lease benefits, multiple tracts, or all depths. If the legal description or conveyance appears broader than the negotiated deal, stop and obtain appropriate review.

Texas Property Code section 5.021 provides general requirements for a conveyance of an estate in real property. Applying those requirements and interpreting a specific deed are legal questions.

## Convert the headline amount into expected net terms

Ask the buyer to state every fact that can change the purchase amount. Common variables can include confirmed acreage, ownership fraction, royalty decimal, title exceptions, excluded wells or tracts, deductions, curative costs, and the buyer's final diligence decision.

For each adjustment, require four elements:

1. the event or finding that triggers it;
2. the evidence used to establish the finding;
3. the exact calculation method; and
4. the seller's right to review, dispute, or terminate.

If two offers cover different interests or use different adjustment rights, their headline numbers are not directly comparable.

## Negotiate diligence and exclusivity together

Diligence gives a buyer time to investigate. Exclusivity can restrict the seller during that period. Review:

- the start and end dates;
- automatic or optional extensions;
- documents the seller must provide;
- title or diligence objections the buyer may raise;
- the time allowed for a response or cure;
- the buyer's right to reduce the price or withdraw;
- the seller's termination rights; and
- what happens to confidential records if the deal ends.

An exclusivity period without a defined buyer workload or closing obligation can shift control to one side. The appropriate balance depends on the specific transaction and qualified legal guidance.

## Negotiate closing certainty

The agreement should connect the final deed with verified payment. Ask:

- Which document transfers the interest?
- Who prepares and approves the final deed?
- Who must sign and acknowledge it?
- When is the deed delivered or released?
- When are funds sent and available?
- Who records the deed?
- What happens if the buyer does not fund or a condition fails?
- Are any funds held back after closing?

Avoid relying on an oral promise that payment will follow later. The closing sequence belongs in the written agreement and instructions.

## Review assignment and buyer identity

Record the legal buyer, authorized signer, address, and payment source. The [Texas Comptroller's public databases](https://comptroller.texas.gov/transparency/open-data/cpa-databases/) can help check entity information, but the search is not an endorsement or proof of capacity.

If the agreement permits assignment, ask whether notice or consent is required and whether the original buyer remains liable. Assignment language can affect who performs, funds, or acquires the interest.

## Review representations, warranties, indemnity, and survival

A seller may be asked to make statements about ownership, authority, liens, leases, litigation, taxes, prior transfers, production, or other facts. Compare each statement with the evidence the seller actually has.

Then review the remedy if a statement is disputed: price reduction, repayment, indemnity, offset, litigation, or another remedy. Determine which obligations survive closing and for how long. These are legal-risk terms, not boilerplate to ignore.

## Use public data without turning it into a promise

The [Railroad Commission](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/oil-and-gas/research-and-statistics/production-data/) publishes operator-reported production context. That data can help test assumptions, but it does not prove title, net acreage, lease burdens, future production, or a fair transaction price.

The [MRX methodology](/methodology/) describes a directional review of production history, royalty income, decline context, lease or offer terms, and stated assumptions. It is not a certified appraisal, legal opinion, or tax opinion.

## Build a clause-by-clause counteroffer

Create a short table with four columns: contract section, current term, concern, and proposed revision. Prioritize the terms that could change what is sold, expected net proceeds, closing certainty, or post-closing exposure.

Useful counteroffer topics include:

1. exact property and interest;
2. stated price and adjustment formula;
3. fees, deductions, and holdbacks;
4. diligence and title objections;
5. exclusivity and extensions;
6. termination and remedies;
7. assignment;
8. deed form and closing instructions;
9. representations and warranties;
10. indemnity and survival; and
11. confidentiality and document handling.

## Know when to pause

Pause when the buyer will not identify the legal entity, the interest cannot be described consistently, the price can change without a calculable method, the deed is broader than the negotiated sale, the closing sequence separates document delivery from verified payment, or the seller does not understand a surviving obligation.

Pausing is not rejecting the deal. It is creating space for the evidence and qualified review needed to make the next decision.
