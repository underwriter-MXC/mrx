## What to Do When You Have Multiple Offers for Your Mineral Rights

> Multiple offers for your mineral rights create real leverage, but only when you compare them against an independent benchmark. The right move is to build a written comparison framework, ask every buyer the same set of questions, and confirm the math against a published DCF methodology before you sign anything.

### Key Takeaways

- **Multiple offers create leverage only when you can compare them apples-to-apples**: An offer sheet that quotes a single dollar amount without disclosing the underlying multiple, discount rate, or assumed decline is not directly comparable to another sheet that does.
- **An independent benchmark is the anchor**: Comparing offers to a documented DCF valuation, built on the [MRX published methodology](/methodology/) and public production data, is the most reliable way to determine which offer is actually competitive.
- **Royalty income multiple is the most overlooked number**: The implied multiple, the offer divided by your trailing monthly royalty income, is a simple cross-check that surfaces offers worth a deeper look. Ask every buyer to disclose it.
- **Inheritance and probate add a second layer of decisions**: If the interest was inherited, the federal stepped-up basis under [26 USC § 1014](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1014) and [IRS Publication 551](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-publication-551) govern the tax mechanics. The comparison framework still applies, but the tax conversation belongs with a qualified tax advisor.
- **Texas regulatory context matters**: The [Texas Railroad Commission](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/) maintains the public production records that any credible buyer will use to value your interest. If a buyer is not pulling from RRC, ask why.

## Why Multiple Offers Are Normal in Mineral Rights

In most local real estate markets, an owner who receives multiple unsolicited offers has one of two reactions: suspicion that the market is hot, or suspicion that the offers are coordinated. In mineral rights, multiple offers are the norm, not the exception, because the buyer universe is small, the pricing model is well-defined, and unsolicited mail campaigns are a standard landman acquisition channel.

If you receive multiple offers, the question is how to compare them.

## Build a Written Comparison Framework

The first step is to put every offer into the same structure. The structure below is also the structure used by the [MRX offer review pillar](/offer-review/).

| Component | What to ask every buyer |
|---|---|
| Total dollar amount | Net to seller, after all deductions, fees, and post-closing adjustments |
| Price per net mineral acre | Calculated from your net mineral acres, not quoted as a single number |
| Implied royalty income multiple | The offer divided by your trailing monthly royalty income |
| Closing timeline | Days from contract to funded sale |
| Financing contingency | Whether the buyer's funds are committed or are subject to a financing condition |
| Due diligence clawback | Whether the buyer can reduce the price after the contract is signed |
| Title requirements | What title standards the buyer requires and who pays for title insurance |
| Lease / operator consent | Whether the operator must approve the assignment |
| Mineral owner signature requirements | All owners, or just majority; spousal joinder required |

A buyer who refuses to fill in this table is telling you that the offer is not yet ready to compare.

## Anchor the Comparison to an Independent Valuation

Offers are outputs. The independent valuation is the input. The valuation should be built on:

- Public production data from the [RRC](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/) and the [EIA Texas profile](https://www.eia.gov/state/?id=TX).
- The [MRX published methodology](/methodology/) for DCF construction, comparable-transaction selection, and royalty multiple review.
- A defensible value range, not a single point estimate.

Once the range is in hand, every offer either falls inside, above, or below it. Offers inside the range are competitive on price. Offers above the range may be overpaying (which sounds good but sometimes signals a buyer recovering costs through clawbacks). Offers below the range warrant direct questions.

## What Multiple Offers Reveal About Your Interest

When multiple buyers compete for the same interest, three signals are worth reading carefully.

- **Convergent offer amounts.** If three or more buyers cluster within a narrow band, the offers share a common pricing reference. Any buyer meaningfully below the cluster warrants a direct question about the underlying methodology.
- **Divergent methodologies.** If one offer implies a royalty multiple far above another offer on the same interest, the lower-multiple offer is using a different decline assumption, a different future price deck, or a different comparable-transactions selection. Ask for the assumption table.
- **Divergent timelines.** The buyer who can close in a shorter window is, in many cases, quoting a different price or stricter clawback terms to compensate for the speed. If speed matters and the price gap is documented, the trade can be rational. If speed is offered with no compensating change in price or terms, ask the buyer to confirm the rationale.

## The Inheritance and Probate Layer

If the interest was inherited, the comparison framework applies exactly the same way, but the tax conversation is different. The federal stepped-up basis under [26 USC § 1014](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1014) generally resets the basis to fair market value at the date of death, which can substantially reduce the taxable gain on a subsequent sale. [IRS Publication 551](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-publication-551) covers the basis rules in detail.

A practical implication: two heirs selling the same inherited interest at the same offer price can have materially different after-tax outcomes depending on each heir's individual basis. The negotiation should focus on the gross offer, and the tax planning should happen with a qualified CPA before signing.

For probate-specific situations, the [Texas Comptroller's property tax resources](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/property-tax/) describe how mineral interests are classified as real property in Texas, which affects how the estate is administered.

## Ask Every Buyer the Same Five Questions

The comparison framework is only useful if every buyer answers. The five questions that surface the most information:

1. **What multiple of trailing monthly royalty income is your offer implying?**
2. **What long-term oil and gas price deck did you assume?**
3. **What decline curve and remaining reserve figure did you use?**
4. **What is your due diligence clawback, and what triggers it?**
5. **What is your funding source, and is it committed?**

A buyer who answers all five with documented assumptions is a buyer who can be compared. A buyer who answers one or two and refuses the rest is not ready to be compared.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I compare multiple mineral rights offers?

Convert every offer into the same three numbers: total net dollars to seller, price per net mineral acre, and implied royalty income multiple. Then compare all three against an independent valuation built on the [MRX methodology](/methodology/) and public production data. The offer that is closest to the middle of the valuation range, with the cleanest clawback terms and the shortest closing timeline, is the most competitive.

### Should I take the highest offer for my mineral rights?

Not necessarily. The highest offer is the highest gross number, but the net to seller depends on closing costs, clawback provisions, financing contingencies, and the operator's consent requirements. A $50,000 offer with a 30% clawback ceiling can deliver less than a $42,000 offer with no clawback. Read the contract, not the headline.

### What is a fair royalty multiple for mineral rights?

Within the [MRX published methodology](/methodology/), the implied multiple is one of the cross-checks used to confirm the DCF and comparable-transactions methods are producing a market-consistent answer. The multiple itself is calculated by dividing the offer by the trailing monthly royalty income; the published methodology does not state a fixed market range.

### Do I have to accept the first offer I receive?

No. There is no obligation to accept any offer, and unsolicited offers are by definition the buyer's first attempt at a price. The most effective response is to request an independent underwriter review, share the results with each buyer, and let them sharpen their offers. Sellers who follow this sequence routinely achieve better outcomes than sellers who anchor to the first number.

### What if the offers are all for different net mineral acres?

Confirm your exact net mineral acreage from your lease and division order before comparing offers. Two buyers quoting different prices per acre may be assuming different net acre counts. A 5% difference in assumed acres can move the per-acre price by 5% without the buyer changing the underlying value.

### Can I get a 1031 exchange with multiple offers?

Yes. If you sell and reinvest in like-kind real property within the IRC § 1031 timelines (45-day identification, 180-day closing), you can defer the capital gains on the relinquished sale. See [How to Navigate a 1031 Exchange for Mineral Rights](/blog/how-to-navigate-a-1031-exchange-for-mineral-rights/) for the mechanics. The exchange structure can be applied to the highest and best of the multiple offers.

## The Bottom Line

Multiple offers are leverage, but only if you convert them into a comparable structure and anchor the structure to an independent valuation. The right sequence is: gather the offers, build a single comparison table, request an independent underwriter review, and let the offers compete on the same math.
