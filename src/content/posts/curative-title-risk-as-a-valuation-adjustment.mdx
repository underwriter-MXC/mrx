> This article provides general owner education. It does not provide a title opinion, professional valuation opinion, cure promise, closing commitment, escrow term, or individualized legal, tax, accounting, or investment guidance. MRX may have an economic interest in a mineral transaction. An owner-specific conclusion requires the controlling documents and qualified professional review.

## Answer first

**Treat curative title risk as a documented combination of affected interest, possible cure cost, timing, outcome uncertainty, and residual transfer or payment risk, not as a standard percentage haircut.** Start by naming the exact issue and the evidence that would resolve it. Then model supported scenarios without assuming that every missing document is fatal or that every unresolved issue will be cured.

A useful adjustment answers five separate questions:

1. What exact interest or cash flow could the issue affect?
2. What documentary or procedural path has been identified?
3. What direct cost and third-party steps are supported?
4. How could timing or outcome change payment, development, transfer, or closing?
5. What uncertainty remains after the expected work?

Keeping those questions separate prevents a vague title concern from becoming an unexplained discount. It also prevents the same delay or uncertainty from being counted in several places.

## What curative title risk means

“Curative” generally describes evidence or action requested to address an exception, gap, inconsistency, or open question in a title review. The word does not tell an owner whether the item is minor, whether a particular instrument will work, who ultimately owns the interest, or whether the issue can be resolved on a transaction schedule.

The valuation issue is narrower: how could the unresolved condition change the expected economic benefit, timing, transferability, or execution risk for the interest being analyzed?

That question cannot be answered from a label such as “title problem.” An unreleased lien affecting one record, a missing probate link, an inconsistent legal description, an unrecorded conveyance, a name or entity mismatch, a depth ambiguity, and competing ownership claims can have very different cure paths and consequences. Some issues may require only existing evidence in a different form. Others may require signatures, releases, probate work, corrective instruments, litigation, or a change in the interest being valued.

Treat the review item as an open factual and professional question until the controlling evidence supports more.

## Begin with a precise issue statement

Write the issue in a form that another reviewer could test. A useful statement identifies:

- the person, estate, trust, company, or other entity involved;
- the instrument, missing link, inconsistency, lien, claim, or exception;
- the affected county, survey, abstract, tract, depth, unit, or legal description;
- the potentially affected mineral, royalty, executive, leasehold, or other interest;
- the ownership fraction or payment stream at issue, if supported;
- the time period or transaction step affected; and
- the evidence reviewed and the evidence still missing.

Avoid conclusions hidden inside the wording. “No recorded probate document found in the reviewed county index as of the search date” is different from “the heirs do not own the minerals.” “Two deeds use different tract descriptions” is different from “the later deed is invalid.” The first version records an evidence gap; the second asserts a legal result that the search alone may not establish.

Texas recording law illustrates why precision matters. [Texas Property Code Chapter 13](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PR/pdf/PR.13.pdf) addresses the validity of unrecorded instruments against certain creditors or purchasers and states that a properly recorded instrument in the proper county provides notice of its existence and is subject to public inspection. Those rules make the county record essential evidence. They do not make a search result, by itself, an owner-specific title opinion.

[Texas Property Code Chapter 5](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PR/pdf/PR.5.pdf) contains statutory rules governing conveyances of real property. A reviewer still must examine the actual granting language, reservations, exceptions, acknowledgments, descriptions, and related instruments rather than treating the document caption or index entry as the complete conveyance.

## Classify the issue before modeling it

A practical classification helps keep assumptions visible.

### Evidence or identity gap

Examples can include a name variation, entity-status question, missing recorded copy, incomplete address or taxpayer record, or a request for identification evidence. The important questions are what evidence has been requested, whether it already exists, who can provide it, and whether it affects ownership or only payment administration.

### Chain-of-title or conveyance question

Examples can include an unrecorded deed, inconsistent legal descriptions, an ambiguous reservation, overlapping conveyances, a missing link, or uncertainty about the tract, depth, or fraction conveyed. These issues may change what interest is being valued, not merely the cost of paperwork.

### Estate, probate, or succession question

A death in the chain can raise questions about wills, intestacy, personal representatives, devisees, heirs, debts, administration, and the instruments needed in the relevant counties. [Texas Estates Code Chapter 101](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/ES/pdf/ES.101.pdf) states that estate property generally vests immediately at death in devisees or heirs, subject to Section 101.051 and administration-related provisions. That statutory framework is more nuanced than assuming that a missing probate filing means no interest passed or that vesting alone proves a transaction-ready record.

### Lien, adverse claim, or dispute

An unreleased lien, adverse claim, competing chain, lawsuit, or signed-but-contested instrument may require different evidence, parties, authority, and timing than a routine documentation request. Scenario analysis should preserve that difference instead of assigning all title items the same probability and duration.

### Payment or division-order requirement

A payor may identify a title or identity requirement that affects suspense or future payment. [Texas Natural Resources Code Section 91.402](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/NR/pdf/NR.91.pdf) provides payment-timing rules and circumstances in which payment can be withheld without interest, subject to the statute and governing agreement. Those circumstances include a title dispute, reasonable doubt about clear title to proceeds, and an unsatisfied title-opinion requirement after a reasonable curative request. The statute supplies payment context; it does not determine private ownership or prescribe a valuation discount.

## Build the curative title risk worksheet

Use one row for each distinct issue. Do not combine several exceptions into a single “title risk” line if they affect different interests or have different cure paths.

### Identity and scope

Record the issue ID, date identified, source of the requirement, affected owner or entity, county, tract or legal-description reference, depth if relevant, interest type, and the supported affected fraction or cash-flow source. If the affected share is unknown, say so; do not assume the entire property is impaired.

### Evidence reviewed

List each deed, lease, amendment, assignment, probate record, affidavit, judgment, lien, release, division order, title requirement, payor letter, tax record, or regulatory record reviewed. Include recording information and the access date where appropriate. Store sensitive documents securely and share redacted working copies when full identifiers are unnecessary.

The [Texas General Land Office search collections](https://www.glo.texas.gov/archives-and-heritage/search-our-collections) provide access to historical land-grant and map resources. Those records can be important for Texas land history and survey context. They supplement rather than replace county records, controlling private instruments, current title work, or professional conclusions about a particular interest.

### Requested path and dependencies

Record the exact evidence or action requested, the party expected to provide it, the professional or public-office step involved, required signatures or authority, and dependencies. A proposed affidavit, corrective deed, lien release, probate filing, entity certificate, or court action should remain a proposed path until a qualified reviewer confirms that it fits the facts.

### Timing and status

Use a dated status with a range or scenarios, not an unsupported completion promise. Separate owner-controlled tasks from third-party steps such as locating signers, obtaining certified records, professional drafting, public filing, payor review, buyer review, or court scheduling. Record the source for any expected time range and update it when new evidence arrives.

### Cost and economic effect

Keep direct third-party cost estimates separate from transaction or payment effects. Record whether the issue could affect royalty suspense, lease bonus, transferability, closing conditions, escrow or holdback discussions, development participation, or the interest definition itself. Do not turn a preliminary quote or one party’s reserve into a universal valuation fact.

## Translate the worksheet into valuation scenarios

The adjustment should follow the evidence rather than precede it. A transparent analysis can separate five components.

### 1. Direct cure cost

Use supported estimates for record copies, filing, professional work, searches, signatures, releases, or other identified tasks. State who is assumed to bear the cost. Do not include a cost in the value adjustment if it is already paid, reimbursed, allocated elsewhere, or embedded in another transaction term.

### 2. Timing effect

A delay matters economically when it changes the timing of expected cash flow or closing. Model the affected cash flow during the supported period, not the entire mineral value by default. Distinguish suspended royalty payments that may later be released from income that may never be attributable to the interest.

The [Texas Comptroller Manual for Discounting Oil and Gas Income](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/property-tax/docs/96-1703.pdf) documents an income-discounting framework for Texas oil and gas property-tax appraisal, including production, price, expenses, taxes, decline, reserves, discounting, and risk. It supports the general principle that amount, timing, and risk should be explicit. It is not a private-sale pricing formula, a cure-cost schedule, or an owner-specific conclusion.

### 3. Outcome uncertainty

If the cure can lead to more than one supported result, model scenarios. Examples can include a documentation-complete scenario, a delayed-completion scenario, a changed-interest scenario, and an unresolved scenario. State the evidence for any probability rather than choosing one because it makes the calculation work.

When probabilities are not supportable, show the scenario values without blending them into a single expected value. That is more honest than attaching precision to an unknown legal or factual outcome.

### 4. Affected-interest scope

Apply the scenario only to the interest, tract, depth, fraction, period, or cash flow that the issue could affect. A title exception involving one predecessor or one tract may not impair an unrelated interest. Conversely, an ambiguity in the granting or reservation language may change the interest definition and cannot be reduced to clerical cost alone.

### 5. Residual risk

After the anticipated work, ask what remains unresolved. A recorded corrective instrument may address one inconsistency but leave an adverse claim, authority question, or different tract description open. Any residual adjustment needs its own evidence and must not duplicate the direct cost, delay, or outcome uncertainty already modeled.

## Avoid double counting

Title-risk models often become overstated because one concern appears several times. Common overlaps include:

- subtracting the full direct cure cost and also capitalizing the same cost into a broad discount;
- discounting delayed cash flow and then applying another percentage for the identical delay;
- modeling the probability of losing an affected fraction and also excluding that fraction from the base case;
- reducing value for royalty suspense as though the payment disappears, then also assuming the same accrued amount is released after cure;
- treating buyer holdback terms as an independent loss when those terms already respond to the modeled uncertainty; and
- applying a property-wide adjustment to an issue limited to one tract, depth, owner, or period.

Use an adjustment bridge that starts with the value under a clearly defined title assumption and shows each non-overlapping change. If the base value already excludes the affected interest, do not subtract it again.

## Do not treat administrative evidence as a title conclusion

Owners often have division orders, royalty statements, tax records, lease records, or years of payments. Those records matter. They can show how a payor has administered an account, which decimal it used, what property identifiers appeared, and when payments changed.

They do not necessarily prove every conveyance, reservation, inheritance, lien status, tract, depth, or current fraction. A new payor, buyer, lender, or title reviewer may request different evidence or apply a different title standard.

The Railroad Commission of Texas is another important but limited source. The Commission’s [authority and jurisdiction page](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/rrc-authority-and-jurisdiction/) states that oil and gas leases and agreements, pipeline easements, and royalty payments are outside its jurisdiction. Its [royalties FAQ](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/about-us/faqs/royalties-faq/) likewise explains that lease and royalty matters are private matters beyond Commission authority while providing public information about royalty-payment statutes and data. Regulatory filings can support well, operator, permit, field, or production context; they are not private ownership decisions.

## Assemble an owner review package

Organize the record so the open question can be evaluated without rediscovering the chain each time:

1. Create a one-page issue list with dates, affected interests, current status, and requested next evidence.
2. Build a chronological instrument index with county recording references and links to secure copies.
3. Separate recorded instruments from unrecorded copies, drafts, correspondence, and oral history.
4. Map each instrument to the tract, depth, fraction, and predecessor it appears to affect.
5. Add probate, entity, lien, division-order, and payor materials in separate folders.
6. Preserve the exact title requirement or transaction request rather than paraphrasing it from memory.
7. Record direct-cost evidence, timing dependencies, and scenario assumptions beside the issue they support.
8. Keep a change log when a new record changes the assumed ownership or cure path.

This package does not replace a professional chain, title examination, or transaction review. Its value is traceability: every modeled adjustment points back to a defined issue, source, and assumption.

## When professional review changes the model

Escalate the underlying question before relying on a valuation adjustment when the issue changes who owns the interest, what an instrument conveyed or reserved, whether a lien or adverse claim remains, what probate procedure applies, whether a proposed instrument is effective, or what title standard a transaction requires.

The valuation model should update after that review, not attempt to substitute for it. A professional conclusion may narrow the affected interest, identify a different cure path, show that an assumed defect is not material, or reveal that a routine-cost model understates a real ownership dispute.

MRX can organize transaction and valuation evidence and explain the assumptions used in its own review. An owner may still need an independent attorney, landman, accountant, tax professional, petroleum engineer, or other specialist for conclusions within that professional’s scope.

## Put the adjustment in writing

A final valuation note should state:

- the exact issue and affected interest;
- the documents and public records reviewed;
- the current requested cure path and who supplied it;
- the direct-cost evidence and cost-bearing assumption;
- the timing scenarios and affected cash flow;
- the outcome scenarios and any supported probabilities;
- the residual uncertainty after the anticipated work;
- each non-overlapping valuation adjustment; and
- the date, limitations, and professional questions still open.

That record makes the result reviewable. If the facts change, the adjustment can change for an identified reason rather than because someone selected a new percentage.

## Bottom line

Curative title risk can affect mineral value, payment timing, and transaction execution, but the effect is issue-specific. Identify the exact condition, keep evidence gaps separate from legal conclusions, define the affected interest, and separate cure cost, delay, outcome uncertainty, and residual risk.

When the evidence does not support a single result, use scenarios. The goal is not to make uncertainty disappear; it is to show where it enters the analysis and avoid turning a vague title concern into an unsupported valuation haircut.
