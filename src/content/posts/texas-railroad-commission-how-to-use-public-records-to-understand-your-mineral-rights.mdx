## How Texas mineral-rights owners use Railroad Commission public records

The Texas Railroad Commission (RRC) maintains public records that owners and reviewers can use to understand what is happening on or near a mineral interest. Those records describe wells, drilling permits, completions, operators, leases, and reported production. They do not establish who owns the minerals, and they are not a valuation on their own. They are useful, dated inputs that complement the county deed records, the lease, the division order, and the royalty statements.

This article describes what the RRC publishes, what it does not, and how an owner can combine RRC records with county records to assemble a fuller picture of a mineral interest. It also explains how MRX uses RRC data as one of several inputs in its directional reviews, and where a qualified land, title, or tax professional should be consulted.

## What the Texas Railroad Commission publishes

The RRC is the state agency that regulates oil and gas activity in Texas. Among other duties, it collects and publishes records that the public can search online. The RRC publishes:

- **Production records.** Monthly and annual production volumes by well, lease, county, and operator, available through the RRC production-data page and the online research query portal.
- **Permit and drilling-permit records.** Drilling permit applications and dispositions, organized by operator, well, and county.
- **Well records.** Completion reports, plug records, and other well-bore records available through the RRC research portal.
- **Operator and organization records.** Operator names, organization filings, and compliance history.
- **Lease-level records.** Lease names and identifiers as reported by operators, useful for matching royalty statements to specific wells.

These records are available at no cost through the RRC website. Each record carries a publication or retrieval date, an operator name, a county, and a well or lease identifier; an owner can use those fields to assemble a dated history of activity on a specific tract or near it.

## What the RRC does not publish

The RRC does not publish:

- **Mineral ownership records.** Recorded deeds, probate or estate instruments, and other chain-of-title documents are reviewed in the county where the land lies. Leases and division orders answer related contract and payment questions, but the RRC has no ownership file that proves what an owner owns.
- **Royalty fractions or net-revenue-interest decimals.** The royalty share is set in the lease and shown on the division order, and the RRC does not centrally publish these figures for owners.
- **Certified valuations.** RRC data shows production; it does not produce a market value for the underlying mineral interest.

If a question is about ownership, royalty share, or value, RRC public records are an input, not an answer.

## How to assemble an owner-research file using RRC data

A practical research file pairs RRC public data with the documents used to investigate ownership and explain payment. The order below is a common starting point; the actual records available for any given tract depend on its location, age, and lease status.

1. **Identify the tract.** Confirm the county, survey, abstract, or block where the interest sits using the county deed records or an existing deed.
2. **Pull RRC production by lease or well.** Use the RRC online research query to retrieve reported monthly production for the leases that cover the tract. Note the operator name and the lease identifier; these match the labels on royalty statements.
3. **Pull RRC permit and completion records.** Match the wells to drilling permits and completion reports to see when each well was drilled, completed, and (if applicable) plugged.
4. **Cross-check operator history.** Compare the operator on the RRC records to the operator paying royalties. A difference can indicate a change in operator that may not yet be reflected in the owner's records.
5. **Compare to your division order and royalty statements.** Confirm that the decimal interest on the division order matches the lease and that recent royalty statements reflect the production reported to the RRC.
6. **Investigate the recorded chain through the county.** Review the recorded deeds and probate or estate instruments tied to the property. A qualified land or title professional can examine the chain of conveyances where the records are complex or incomplete.

## When to bring in a qualified professional

RRC public records can answer "what is happening on or near my land," but several related questions are outside the RRC's scope:

- **Ownership disputes, severance questions, and chain-of-title review.** These typically require a title opinion from a qualified Texas oil and gas attorney or land professional.
- **Royalty-fraction interpretation and division-order review.** A division-order analyst or oil and gas attorney can compare the lease, the division order, and the deed records.
- **Federal royalty-tax treatment and depletion.** Tax treatment is specific to each owner's facts and is outside an RRC records search. A CPA or tax attorney can address an owner's individual situation.

## Records an owner can request during a review

For a directional MRX review, the following records are commonly useful:

- The current royalty statement(s) for the interest.
- The lease and any amendments.
- The division order(s) issued by the operator.
- Recent (typically 12 months) RRC production for the relevant leases, which MRX can help assemble from public RRC queries.
- County deed records establishing the chain of ownership for the interest.
